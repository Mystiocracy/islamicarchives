History of

Saudi Arabia

10 periods · c. 570 – today. Each period below is a power that ruled all or part of what is now Saudi Arabia.

  1. c. 570–632

    Muhammad and the first community

    Mecca and Medina

    The Prophet Muhammad was born in Mecca around 570. Revelation began around 610. The Hijra, the move to Medina in 622, marks year 1 of the Islamic calendar. He died in 632 after the whole of Arabia had come under the new community.

    Rulers: Muḥammad (c. 570–632)

    Further reading ↗

  2. 632–661

    Rashidun Caliphate

    Medina, then Kufa

    The first four caliphs, Abū Bakr, ʿUmar, ʿUthmān and ʿAlī, led the community. Syria, Iraq, Egypt and Iran came under Muslim rule. The Qurʾān was compiled in written form and the first civil wars began.

    Rulers: Abū Bakr (632–634) · ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb (634–644) · ʿUthmān ibn ʿAffān (644–656) · ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib (656–661)

    Further reading ↗

  3. 750–1258

    Abbasid Caliphate

    Baghdad

    The caliphate of the great age of translation, law, theology and science. Real power passed to regional sultans from the ninth century. The Mongols sacked Baghdad in 1258 and killed the last caliph there.

    Rulers: al-Saffāḥ (750–754) · al-Manṣūr (754–775) · Hārūn al-Rashīd (786–809) · al-Maʾmūn (813–833) · al-Muʿtaṣim (833–842) · al-Mutawakkil (847–861) · al-Mustaʿṣim (1242–1258)

    Further reading ↗

  4. 909–1171

    Fatimid CaliphateHejaz

    Mahdia, then Cairo

    An Ismāʿīlī Shiʿi caliphate that claimed descent from Fāṭima. It ruled North Africa, Egypt and parts of Syria and Arabia. Cairo was founded in 969 and al-Azhar around 970.

    Rulers: ʿUbayd Allāh al-Mahdī (909–934) · al-Muʿizz (953–975) · al-ʿAzīz (975–996) · al-Ḥākim (996–1021) · al-Mustanṣir (1036–1094) · al-ʿĀḍid (1160–1171)

    Further reading ↗

  5. 1250–1517

    Mamluk SultanateHejaz

    Cairo

    Slave-soldier sultans who defeated the Mongols at ʿAyn Jālūt in 1260. They sheltered an Abbasid caliph in Cairo as a figurehead. Ibn Taymiyya, Ibn Khaldūn and al-Suyūṭī lived under their rule.

    Rulers: Baybars (1260–1277) · Qalāwūn (1279–1290) · al-Nāṣir Muḥammad (1293–1341) · Qāytbāy (1468–1496) · Qānṣūh al-Ghawrī (1501–1516)

    Further reading ↗

  6. c. 1299–1922

    Ottoman Empire

    Bursa, Edirne, Istanbul

    Began as a small principality in Anatolia and conquered Constantinople in 1453. After taking Syria and Egypt in 1516–17 it ruled most of the Arab world and the Balkans. The sultanate was abolished in 1922 and the caliphate on 3 March 1924.

    Rulers: Osman I (c. 1299–1324) · Mehmed II (1444–1446, 1451–1481) · Selim I (1512–1520) · Suleiman the Magnificent (1520–1566) · Abdulmejid I (1839–1861) · Abdulhamid II (1876–1909) · Mehmed VI (1918–1922) · Abdulmejid II (1922–1924)

    Further reading ↗

  7. 1744–1932

    Saudi states

    Dirʿiyya, Riyadh

    The alliance between Muḥammad ibn Saʿūd and Muḥammad ibn ʿAbd al-Wahhāb founded the first Saudi state in 1744. A second state followed from 1824 and the modern kingdom was proclaimed in 1932.

    Rulers: Muḥammad ibn Saʿūd (1744–1765) · ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz ibn Saʿūd (1932–1953)

    Further reading ↗

  8. 1914–1946

    End of empire

    Istanbul, Ankara, Cairo

    The Ottoman Empire fell after the First World War. Britain and France took mandates over Arab lands. Turkey abolished the caliphate in 1924, and independence movements grew.

    Rulers: Mustafa Kemal Atatürk (1923–1938) · Reza Shah Pahlavi (1925–1941) · ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz ibn Saʿūd (1932–1953)

  9. 1947–1978

    New states and revival

    Karachi, Cairo, Jakarta

    Pakistan and Indonesia and most Arab and African states won independence. Islamic revival movements such as the Muslim Brotherhood and Jamāʿat-i Islāmī grew. The Organisation of Islamic Cooperation was founded in 1969.

    Rulers: Muhammad Ali Jinnah (1947–1948) · Sukarno (1945–1967) · Gamal Abdel Nasser (1956–1970) · Faisal ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz (1964–1975)

  10. 2000–today

    Contemporary voices

    Worldwide

    Writing in the age of globalisation and the internet. The Arab uprisings began in 2010–11, and Muslim intellectuals now write for global audiences in many languages.
