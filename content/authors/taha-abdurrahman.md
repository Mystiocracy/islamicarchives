Ṭāhā ʿAbd al-Raḥmān (also Taha Abderrahmane or Taha Abdurrahman) is a Moroccan philosopher who works on logic, the philosophy of language, Islamic ethics and the critique of modernity. Across his career he has tried to build a philosophical framework on Islamic ethical foundations, in conversation with modern Western philosophy rather than in opposition to it. His basic claim is that intellectual and civilisational progress cannot be separated from moral and spiritual development.

## Early life and education

He was born in 1944 in El Jadida, on Morocco's Atlantic coast, during the French protectorate. He finished primary school there and, alongside it, learned the basics of the Islamic sciences and ethics from his father. He completed his secondary schooling in Casablanca, then read philosophy at Mohammed V University in Rabat, where he took his first degree.

He went on to the Sorbonne, where he took a second degree in philosophy and, in 1972, a doctorate (*doctorat du troisième cycle*) with a thesis on the linguistic structures underlying the study of being (*al-lugha wa-l-falsafa: risāla fī l-binyāt al-lughawiyya li-mabḥath al-wujūd*). It was published in French in 1979. In 1985 he completed a *doctorat d'État* with a thesis on argumentative and natural reasoning (*Risāla fī l-istidlāl al-ḥijājī wa-l-ṭabīʿī wa-namādhijihi*). That training in classical philosophical questions and modern logical method underlies his later work on renewing Islamic thought.

## Academic career

From 1970 until his retirement in 2005 he taught logic and the philosophy of language at Mohammed V University in Rabat. His research drew on formal logic, linguistics, argumentation theory, Islamic theology and moral philosophy. He did not treat the Islamic heritage only as history, but as a source of concepts and methods for present use. Through lectures and conferences in universities across the Arab world, and later in Turkey and elsewhere, he took part in debates on philosophy, religion, ethics and modernity.

## Thought

His central concern is how reason, morality and religion relate. He argues that reason is more than an abstract, technical faculty: it reaches its fuller sense when joined to moral conduct, spiritual discipline and responsible action. Three themes run through his work.

**Renewing Islamic reason.** He holds that the theological and philosophical traditions of Islam can be renewed by taking up modern logic, linguistic analysis and the theory of argumentation, without giving up their own foundations. His early books treat theology as dialogue and argument, and ask how the Arabic-Islamic heritage should be read and assessed.

**An ethical critique of modernity.** He rejects the assumption that Western history supplies the only valid model of modernity. He separates the "spirit" of modernity from its particular Western form, and argues that other civilisations can arrive at modernities shaped by their own moral and intellectual traditions.

**Trusteeship (*al-iʾtimāniyya*).** His best-known idea is that human beings hold their responsibilities as a trust from God. On this view morality is not an independent system that religion may or may not endorse, and the separation of ethics from religion (*al-dahrāniyya*, in his coinage) leads in the end to the loss of ethics itself. Moral accountability and sincerity of conduct sit at the centre of individual and collective life.

His upbringing in the Sufi tradition also shapes his view of how knowledge relates to action. He is known for coining Arabic philosophical terms, which makes his prose demanding and has drawn a loyal readership among students of philosophy.

## Major works

All are in Arabic.

- *Fī Uṣūl al-Ḥiwār wa-Tajdīd ʿIlm al-Kalām* (1987), on the foundations of dialogue and the renewal of theology, treating dialogue and argument as the basis of theological discourse.
- *Al-ʿAmal al-Dīnī wa-Tajdīd al-ʿAql* (1989), on how religious practice and spiritual experience renew reason.
- *Tajdīd al-Manhaj fī Taqwīm al-Turāth* (1994), which criticises fragmentary and ideological readings of the heritage and proposes an integrated method.
- *al-Lisān wa-l-Mīzān aw al-Takawthur al-ʿAqlī* (1998), *Language and Balance, or Rational Multiplication*, on argumentation and natural-language reasoning, presenting reason as plural rather than a single formal faculty.
- *Suʾāl al-Akhlāq* (2000), *The Question of Ethics*, an ethical critique of Western modernity.
- *Rūḥ al-Ḥadātha* (2006), *The Spirit of Modernity*, on the grounds for an Islamic modernity.
- *Rūḥ al-Dīn* (2012), *The Spirit of Religion*, which develops trusteeship and takes up religion, secularism and politics.
- *Buʾs al-Dahrāniyya* (2014), *The Misery of Secularism*, a critique of separating morality from religion.
- *Dīn al-Ḥayāʾ* (2017), *The Religion of Modesty*, in three volumes, applying the trusteeship approach to ethics and jurisprudence.

## Recognition

He received the ISESCO Prize for Islamic thought and philosophy in 2006. His work is widely discussed by students of Islamic philosophy, theology and ethics, and it has drawn criticism as well as admiration, particularly over how he relates religious morality to philosophical reason and political modernity.

## Legacy

He treats Islamic ethics as a comprehensive philosophical framework, not only as religious instruction, and uses it to address present-day intellectual and social questions. He does not call for rejecting modern philosophy; he asks that its assumptions be reconsidered and its foundations rebuilt from an Islamic ethical standpoint. His project is widely seen as one of the most systematic contemporary attempts to do this.
