Malek Bennabi was born in Constantine, in French-ruled Algeria, in 1905 and grew up partly in Tébessa. He attended Qurʾanic school and then French schools before completing the *médersa* of Constantine in 1925. In 1930 he went to Paris and trained as an electrical engineer, but colonial restrictions kept him from a career in the field. He spent most of the next quarter-century in France, where he married a French convert to Islam and turned to writing on the condition of Muslim societies.

His first book, *Le Phénomène coranique* (1946), defended the revealed character of the Qurʾan in a modern idiom. In *Les Conditions de la renaissance* (1949) and *Vocation de l'Islam* (1954) he set out the ideas for which he is best known: that a civilisation forms from the combination of human beings, land and time under the impulse of a religious idea, and that the Muslim world's subjugation reflected an inner weakness he called *colonisabilité*, the readiness to be colonised, rather than colonial power alone.

In 1956 he moved to Cairo, where he took part in the Afro-Asian movement, wrote *L'Afro-asiatisme*, and saw his books translated into Arabic, which became his main language of publication. Works from this period, including *Le Problème de la culture*, *La Lutte idéologique* and *Naissance d'une société*, examined culture, ideas and social relations as the building blocks of renewal. After Algerian independence he returned home in 1963 and served as director of higher education until 1967, while hosting study circles that shaped a generation of Algerian and Arab activists.

Bennabi died in Algiers in 1973. His civilisational analysis, which stressed moral and intellectual reform over political slogans, remains widely read across North Africa, the Arab world and Malaysia, and his memoirs *Mémoires d'un témoin du siècle* are an important source on colonial Algeria.
