Sayyid Quṭb

سيد قطب

1906–1966 · Egypt · Writer, literary critic, Qurʾan commentator, Islamist ideologue

Sayyid Quṭb was born in 1906 in the village of Mūshā in Asyut province, Upper Egypt. After village schooling he moved to Cairo and graduated from Dār al-ʿUlūm, the teacher-training college, in 1933. For most of the next two decades he worked for the Ministry of Education while making a name as a poet, novelist and literary critic in the circle of ʿAbbās Maḥmūd al-ʿAqqād. His studies al-Taṣwīr al-fannī fī al-Qurʾān (1945) and Mashāhid al-qiyāma fī al-Qurʾān approached the Qurʾan through its literary imagery, and his memoir Ṭifl min al-qarya (1946) described rural Egyptian childhood.

From the late 1940s his writing turned decisively to Islam as a social and political programme. al-ʿAdāla al-ijtimāʿiyya fī al-Islām (1949) presented Islam as the basis of a just social order distinct from both capitalism and communism. Between 1948 and 1950 he studied in the United States, an experience that sharpened his criticism of Western society. In the early 1950s he joined the Muslim Brotherhood and edited its publications.

After the Brotherhood’s clash with Nasser’s government in 1954 he spent most of the following decade in prison. There he completed his multi-volume Qurʾan commentary Fī ẓilāl al-Qurʾān and wrote short works such as Hādhā al-dīn and Khaṣāʾiṣ al-taṣawwur al-Islāmī. His best-known book, Maʿālim fī al-ṭarīq (Milestones, 1964), argued that contemporary Muslim societies had relapsed into jāhiliyya and called for a committed vanguard to restore God’s sovereignty (ḥākimiyya). Released in 1964, he was arrested again in 1965, tried for conspiracy, and hanged on 29 August 1966.

Quṭb is among the most widely read and most contested Muslim thinkers of the twentieth century. His commentary remains popular with general readers, while his later political ideas have been debated by mainstream Islamists, critics and scholars of radical movements alike.
