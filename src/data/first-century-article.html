1. What the article sets out to do

Only one surviving Qur'an manuscript carries a date from the first century of the hijra, and only two from the second. Everything else has to be dated by other means. The Islamic Awareness article gathers the manuscripts that can reasonably be placed in that first century, roughly the seventh century CE, and gives references for each. These are copies in the early ḥijāzī script and in early kufic.

The scope is deliberately narrow. Qur'anic inscriptions on rock, coins and buildings are dealt with on a separate page. The authors call the list a working document and expect to keep adding to it.

Why stop at the end of the first century? Any cut-off is somewhat arbitrary, they admit, but this one makes sense. The late first century is when Arabic became the language of government, when the great Umayyad buildings went up, and when Qur'ans began to be copied under official patronage.

2. Earlier attempts at a list

The article builds on three predecessors. Adolf Grohmann, writing in the 1950s, dated early Qur'an fragments by comparing their script with dated Arabic papyri. His list has eight entries but only seven distinct manuscripts, since two of them belong together. Beatrice Gruendler's 1993 survey of dated first-century Arabic texts added three more: the Mingana palimpsest, part of Codex Ṣanʿāʾ I, and a Kuwaiti fragment of the British Library's Or. 2165.

The most recent and fullest effort before this one was a table of ḥijāzī manuscripts by Marc-Georges Noseda and François Déroche. Noseda reckoned that, taken together, these manuscripts preserve about 83% of the Qur'anic text. The Islamic Awareness authors found the table useful but thin. It names holding libraries and gives a rough idea of contents, with no bibliography, and they set out to fill that gap.

3. How the manuscripts are dated

The authors argue that no single technique will do, and that a date is only as good as the agreement between several kinds of evidence. They also reject the idea that only a manuscript with a dated endowment note can be trusted. Very few early fragments would ever meet that test.

3.1 What the early writers say

The tenth-century bibliographer Ibn al-Nadīm names Makkah, Madīnah, Baṣra and Kūfa as the homes of the earliest scripts, and describes the Makkan and Madinan alif as turned to the right, tall and slightly slanted. Following Estelle Whelan, the authors take this as a description of the script as a whole rather than just one letter. Because Makkan and Madinan hands cannot in practice be told apart, "ḥijāzī" has become the catch-all name for the earliest Qur'anic script.

A second source is Yaḥyā ibn Abī Kathīr (d. 132/749), who recalled the order in which reading aids were added to the muṣḥaf. First came dots to tell similar letters apart, then dots at verse ends, then markers for where sūrahs begin and end. He says this in passing, without any axe to grind in the dotting debate, which the authors take as a sign that he is reliable.

From this they suggest four rough stages, from no marks at all up to letter dots, verse markers and sūrah markers together. The stages overlapped and scribes were conservative, so a manuscript can't simply be slotted into one. Every manuscript on their list has light dotting and some verse markers. The bare earliest stages, they think, have either not survived or not yet been found.

3.2 Script and book-making

Déroche divided ḥijāzī into four types, I to IV, and dated ḥijāzī Qur'ans to the first century, with kufic gradually taking over after ʿAbd al-Malik's reforms. The authors use his framework but have doubts about it. It is not clear whether the small differences between his types reflect region, period or just individual scribes, and the type labels are a modern classification rather than anything the manuscripts themselves suggest.

On the physical side, nearly all early Qur'ans are parchment codices, with a few rolls. Most were made up of quires of ten leaves, but not all: Arabe 328a, from the same period as the ten-leaf Arabe 328c, uses quires of eight.

Colour is another clue. Red sūrah dividers appear in Chester Beatty Is. 1615 I and in Arabe 328c / Mingana 1572a, and TIEM ŞE 362 uses three colours in a geometric band. The authors use these examples against the assumption that any decorated Qur'an must be late.

3.3 Comparison with dated art

Decoration can also be matched against dated buildings. The great illuminated Qur'an DAM 20-33.1 has been compared with the mosaics of the Dome of the Rock and the Umayyad mosques of Ṣanʿāʾ and Damascus, and Hans-Caspar Graf von Bothmer placed it around 710–715 CE, under al-Walīd. Déroche dated TIEM ŞE 321 to the last quarter of the first century on similar grounds. Critics point out a risk of circular reasoning here, since most of the comparison material is itself Umayyad.

3.4 Radiocarbon dating

Radiocarbon dating has the great advantage of owing nothing to theories about how the script developed. The authors count nineteen Qur'an manuscripts dated this way (see their radiocarbon page), eight of them in their list. The weakness is that some results come with very wide ranges. The St Petersburg E20 and the Samarqand codex, both traditionally but wrongly linked to ʿUthmān, span 225 and 260 years. Others are much tighter:

4. How sure can we be?

This section is a pushback against what the authors see as excessive caution. Their example is A. J. Arberry, who put Chester Beatty Is. 1615 I in the fourth century. A late date may look like the safe choice, they say, but if the manuscript is really much older it is simply wrong. Errors in either direction distort the history of the script, and so the dating of every other manuscript measured against it.

They accept Sheila Blair's point that there is no absolute method for dating Qur'ans before the ninth century CE. But historical certainty, they reply, was never the same as mathematical proof. When script analysis and radiocarbon point the same way, as they do for Mingana 1572a, a first-century date is well supported. They compare the situation with early Arab-Byzantine coins, which went undated for a long time until a methodological breakthrough settled the question.

5. Where the manuscripts came from

Many of the manuscripts on the list sit in Western libraries, though none was made there. None is known from present-day Saudi Arabia, and the authors put that down to a lack of searching and cataloguing rather than a genuine absence. Their account of how the material was gathered is pointed, and readers should weigh it against other scholarship:

  • The German photographs. They say Gotthelf Bergsträsser and Otto Pretzl photographed some 15,000 pages of early Qur'ans on their travels without sharing the images with their hosts. Anton Spitaler inherited the archive and, the article claims, kept it hidden for more than fifty years before handing it to Angelika Neuwirth, who now leads the Corpus Coranicum project.
  • Ṣanʿāʾ photographs. A German conservation team in Yemen also photographed the Ṣanʿāʾ fragments. According to the article, an attempt to take about 35,000 images out of the country was stopped until German diplomats intervened. Yemen holds the master microfilms and the copyright.
  • Damascus. After a fire at the Great Mosque in 1893, a forgotten store of worn-out Qur'ans came to light. What survived was sent to Istanbul, where two French scholars rediscovered it in 1963. Déroche puts the collection at around 210,000 leaves, mostly Qur'anic.
  • Ṣanʿāʾ. In 1965 rain damaged the Great Mosque of Ṣanʿāʾ, and workers found a sealed room full of old manuscripts, nearly all of them Qur'ans. Some leaves were sold off; the rest went to the National Museum in 1972. The hoard holds close to 1,000 different Qur'ans in about 15,000 fragments, and only a small part has been published.

6. How much of the Qur'an survives

To put a figure on the value of these manuscripts, the authors measure them against the printed Madinah muṣḥaf (the Ḥafṣ reading), much as Noseda used the King Fuʾād edition. The Madinah muṣḥaf has 604 pages of fifteen lines and 6,236 verses. The sūrahs shrink quickly in length: half the text is done by al-Kahf (18) and about nine-tenths by al-Mujādila (58), so the last 56 sūrahs make up only a tenth of the whole. Finding a few long sūrahs therefore matters far more than finding many short ones.

What the sūrah table shows

The authors go through the Qur'an sūrah by sūrah, noting which manuscripts contain each one and how much survives. Very fragmentary pieces, P. Michaélidès 32 among them, are counted as witnesses to a sūrah but left out of the verse totals.

Coverage is close to complete. Al-Fātiḥa survives in full. The one big gap among the long sūrahs is al-Baqara, of which 174 of 286 verses (about 61%) are attested. From sūrah 3 onwards nearly everything is present in full, right through to the short sūrahs at the end. Only sūrahs 111, 112 and 113 have no witness at all, so 111 of the 114 sūrahs are represented. For sūrah 110 the source table gives 2 of 3 verses but prints the share as 100%. One of the two must be a slip; we give the verse count as printed.

Added up, the manuscripts attest 6,059 of the 6,236 verses, about 97%. Measured by amount of text rather than number of verses, the figure is a little lower, about 96.1%, or the equivalent of some 580 of the muṣḥaf's 604 pages. Most of what is missing comes from al-Baqara, where the verses are long. The authors see this as a real advance on Noseda's 83%. Noseda had counted only ḥijāzī manuscripts on parchment; their list adds papyri, the Ṣanʿāʾ parchments and early kufic copies.

A surprisingly small number of manuscripts does most of the work. Or. 2165 alone holds about 53% of the text, Arabe 328a about 26% and DAM 01-29.1 about 22%, and between them the three come close to 80%. Several sizeable manuscripts, among them M a VI 165, Arabe 328c, Mingana 1572b and Marcel 19, add nothing the big three don't already cover.

Keith Small objected that much of sūrahs 77–114 is unattested. The authors answer that this stretch is only about 4% of the Qur'an, and that the first and last leaves of a well-used codex are exactly the ones most likely to go missing.

The order of the sūrahs

Wherever a manuscript runs on from the end of one sūrah into the next, the authors checked the order against the printed muṣḥaf. They found 330 such transitions in 33 of the 50 manuscripts, and every one follows the familiar order.

The exception is the erased lower text of Codex Ṣanʿāʾ I, whose twelve surviving transitions follow a different sequence. The authors side with Behnam Sadeghi and Mohsen Goudarzi, who treat that lower text as an independent tradition, not one of the Companion codices known from the literary sources. They also correct Etan Kohlberg and Mohammad Ali Amir-Moezzi, who wrote that 22% of the Ṣanʿāʾ Qur'ans have a different sūrah order. Von Bothmer's 22%, the article points out, referred to manuscripts with a varying number of lines per page, not to sūrah order.

A few further points

  • The first and last sūrahs. Some reports say Ibn Masʿūd did not count al-Fātiḥa as part of the Qur'an. The authors answer with DAM 01-25.1, where al-Fātiḥa runs straight into al-Baqara with nothing before it, and DAM 20-33.1, which has all of al-Fātiḥa and part of al-Nās.
  • The Ṣanʿāʾ palimpsest. They think the two layers of Codex Ṣanʿāʾ I were written no more than a generation apart. To them this shows that, by the middle of the first century, a non-standard text was recognised as such and written over.
  • Decoration and kufic. The lower text of the palimpsest already has ornamental bands between sūrahs, so decoration is no proof of lateness. And if kufic could be carved on early Umayyad milestones and the Dome of the Rock, they see no reason to rule it out on seventh-century parchment.
  • Luxenberg. A short section dismisses Christoph Luxenberg's theory that the Qur'an was first written in Garshuni, Arabic in Syriac letters.

7. The article's conclusions

The authors set their findings against the long argument over when the Qur'anic text was fixed. The traditional account credits ʿUthmān (r. 23–35/644–56) with a standard text. Against it stand a series of revisionist theories: Patricia Crone and Michael Cook's Hagarism, John Wansbrough's late canonisation, and the work of Yehuda Nevo, Uri Rubin, G. R. Hawting and Luxenberg. The authors' complaint is that these theories disagree with each other and were mostly built without looking at the manuscripts.

Their answer is the numbers. First-century manuscripts preserve about 97% of the verses and about 96% of the text, compared with Noseda's 83% for ḥijāzī parchments alone. They are careful to say the work is unfinished. Much material, especially in the Damascus papers (Şam Evrakı) now at the Museum of Turkish and Islamic Art in Istanbul, is still unpublished and could change the figures.

They end on Patricia Crone, who later changed her mind after the radiocarbon dating of Codex Ṣanʿāʾ I and accepted that ʿUthmān's committee did produce a standard text, as the tradition says.

An appendix takes aim at the habit of calling the printed Qur'an the "Vulgate" or "textus receptus". The phrase textus receptus goes back to a 1633 printing of the Greek New Testament, and the authors argue it implies an uncritical text that later scholarship exposed as corrupt. Nothing in the early Qur'an manuscripts, they say, supports that picture, and the two textual histories are too different for the comparison to hold.

9. Other manuscripts the article mentions

Beyond the main table the article lists, more briefly, a long run of other early pieces. In Yemen these are DAM 01-28.1, 01-30.1, 01-18.3, 01-32.1, 01-29.2 and 01-32.2. In Turkey there are Topkapı Medina 1a, several TIEM manuscripts and TIEM ŞE 12995. Vienna has A. Perg. 186, A. Perg. 202 and Mixt. 917. There are also pieces in Tunisia, Egypt, the United States, Britain (many in the Khalili Collection), France and Ireland, the multi-library Codex Amrensis 1, and a good number sold at Sotheby's and Christie's.

The Mingana palimpsest, whose lower text is first-century, has its own page and is due to join the table later. So does the little-studied Kodex Wetzstein II 1913 in Berlin, 210 folios long. The authors also mention a 270-folio ḥijāzī codex on display at the Museum of Islamic Art in Cairo that scholars have barely looked at.

Finally there are the Qur'ans popularly credited to ʿUthmān or ʿAlī: Samarqand, St Petersburg, Topkapı, TIEM, the al-Ḥusayn Mosque and Dār al-Kutub copies in Cairo, and the ʿAlī Qur'an in Ṣanʿāʾ.
