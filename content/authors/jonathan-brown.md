Jonathan Andrew Cleveland Brown (b. 1977) is an American historian of Islam and professor at Georgetown University, where he holds the Alwaleed bin Talal Chair of Islamic Civilization in the School of Foreign Service. [[1]](#ref-1) His work centres on hadith, Islamic law and theology, and on how Muslim scholars have read and applied scripture, from the medieval period to the present.

## Early life and education

Brown was born in Washington, D.C., and raised in the Episcopal Church. He became a Muslim in 1997 while an undergraduate at Georgetown University. [[6]](#ref-6) He took his B.A. in history, magna cum laude, in May 2000, with a minor in Russian and a certificate in Islam and Muslim-Christian Understanding. [[2]](#ref-2)

From June 2000 to June 2001 he studied Arabic at the Center for Arabic Study Abroad in Cairo. He then went to the University of Chicago, where he completed a Ph.D. in Islamic thought in the Department of Near Eastern Languages and Civilizations in August 2006. [[2]](#ref-2) His book on the canonical standing of the two Ṣaḥīḥs grew out of this period of research. [[7]](#ref-7)

## Academic career

Brown was assistant professor of Arabic and Islamic Studies at the University of Washington in Seattle from 2006 to 2010, and in 2010 moved to Georgetown to take up the Alwaleed bin Talal Chair. [[2]](#ref-2) He directed Georgetown's Prince Alwaleed Bin Talal Center for Muslim-Christian Understanding until 2019. [[6]](#ref-6) He is editor-in-chief of the *Oxford Encyclopedia of Islam and Law*, an appointment his CV dates to 2009. [[1]](#ref-1) [[2]](#ref-2) His research has taken him to Egypt, Syria, Turkey, Morocco, Saudi Arabia, Yemen, South Africa, India, Indonesia and Iran. [[4]](#ref-4)

## Scholarly contributions

**Hadith.** *The Canonization of al-Bukhārī and Muslim* (2007) asks how two collections of Prophetic reports came to hold canonical authority in Sunni Islam, and what part scholars, legal schools and theological disputes played in that process. [[7]](#ref-7) *Hadith: Muhammad's Legacy in the Medieval and Modern World* (2009; second edition 2017) is a general account of how hadith were collected, transmitted, graded and interpreted, and of the modern arguments over their authenticity. [[1]](#ref-1) [[2]](#ref-2)

**Interpretation and law.** *Misquoting Muhammad* (2014) looks at how Muslim scholars have read the Prophet's legacy, and at the choices open to those who interpret it today. *Muhammad: A Very Short Introduction* (2011) summarises the sources for the Prophet's life; it was part of the National Endowment for the Humanities' Muslim Journeys Bookshelf. [[5]](#ref-5) [[8]](#ref-8)

**Ethics and history.** *Slavery and Islam* (2019) examines slavery in Islamic law, theology and history, and how Muslims today deal with the inherited rulings. [[9]](#ref-9) *Islam and Blackness* (2022) studies attitudes to Black Africans in Muslim societies and the part played by texts, law and social practice. [[10]](#ref-10)

**Courts and justice.** *Justice and Islamic Law: Mazalim Courts and Legal Reform* (2026) is a study of the *maẓālim* courts, through which rulers heard complaints against officials and the powerful, and of their place beside the sharīʿa judiciary. [[11]](#ref-11)

Brown writes as both an academic historian and a practising Muslim, and he has been associated with the Yaqeen Institute for Islamic Research. [[6]](#ref-6) His books are read by university students and by general Muslim readers.

*Muhammad: A Very Short Introduction* was chosen for the NEH's Bridging Cultures Muslim Journeys Bookshelf. [[5]](#ref-5) Brown is a term member of the Council on Foreign Relations. [[3]](#ref-3) [[6]](#ref-6)

## Intellectual contribution

Brown's work sets out to show how the Islamic scholarly tradition handled its own texts. He pays attention to the differences between schools and periods, and to the gap between what the sources say and what Muslim societies practised. Describing a position in his books is not the same as endorsing it, and his arguments on contested subjects such as slavery, race and legal reform have drawn both support and criticism.
