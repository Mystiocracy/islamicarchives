Rashīd Riḍā

محمد رشيد رضا

1865–1935 · Ottoman Syria (Lebanon); Egypt · Scholar, journalist, Qurʾān commentator, reformer

Muḥammad Rashīd Riḍā was born in 1865 in al-Qalamūn, a village near Tripoli in what is now Lebanon, to a family of sayyids with Sufi connections. He studied at the National Islamic School of Ḥusayn al-Jisr in Tripoli, where Islamic subjects were taught alongside modern ones, and for a time followed the Naqshbandī path. Reading issues of al-ʿUrwa al-wuthqā turned him towards the reform programme of al-Afghānī and Muḥammad ʿAbduh.

In 1897 he moved to Cairo to join ʿAbduh, and the following year founded the journal al-Manār, which he edited until his death. It ran for some 35 volumes and carried essays, fatwas and correspondence from across the Muslim world, from Morocco to Java. Riḍā recorded ʿAbduh’s Qurʾān lectures and continued them on his own after 1905; the result was Tafsīr al-Manār, which reached Sūrat Yūsuf. He also wrote ʿAbduh’s biography and collected his writings.

Over time Riḍā moved closer to Ḥanbalī and Wahhābī positions. He stressed the authority of the Qurʾān and authentic Sunna, opposed popular Sufi practices, and defended the Saudi state. He was also active in politics: he took part in Arab and Syrian nationalist circles, and after the Ottoman sultanate was abolished he proposed a reformed caliphate in al-Khilāfa aw al-imāma al-ʿuẓmā (1922). He sharply attacked ʿAlī ʿAbd al-Rāziq’s case for separating religion and state.

Riḍā died in 1935 on the road back from Suez to Cairo. Al-Manār made him one of the most widely read Muslim writers of his time. His blend of reformist and Salafi thought influenced Ḥasan al-Bannā and many later Islamic movements.
