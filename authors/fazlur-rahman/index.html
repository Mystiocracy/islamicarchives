Fazlur Rahman

فضل الرحمٰن ملک

1919–1988 · British India / Pakistan; United States · Philosopher, Qur'anic scholar, historian of Islamic thought, reformer

Fazlur Rahman Malik was born in 1919 in the Hazara district of the North-West Frontier Province, the son of a religious scholar who taught him the traditional Islamic disciplines at home. He studied Arabic at Punjab University in Lahore and then went to Oxford, where he wrote a doctoral thesis on Ibn Sina’s psychology, published in 1952 as Avicenna’s Psychology. He taught Persian and Islamic philosophy at Durham University and later Islamic studies at McGill University in Montreal.

In the early 1960s he returned to Pakistan at the invitation of Ayub Khan’s government to direct the Central Institute of Islamic Research in Karachi, a body meant to advise on bringing law and policy into line with Islam. His published views on interest, family law, hadith and the nature of revelation provoked fierce opposition from religious parties, and in 1968 he resigned and left the country. From 1969 until his death he taught at the University of Chicago, where he held the Harold H. Swift Distinguished Service Professorship of Islamic Thought.

Rahman argued that Muslims must recover the moral purpose of the Qur’an by reading it historically: first understanding its verses against the circumstances of seventh-century Arabia, then extracting their general ethical principles, and finally applying those principles to present conditions. This “double movement” informed Islamic Methodology in History, Islam, Major Themes of the Qur’an and Islam and Modernity, in which he also criticised both the rigidity of traditional scholarship and the superficiality of much Islamic modernism. His philosophical work included studies of prophecy and of Mulla Sadra.

He died in Chicago in 1988. Through his writings, widely translated in Turkey and Indonesia, and through doctoral students who returned to teach across the Muslim world, he became one of the most influential figures in modern reformist approaches to the Qur’an and Islamic law.
