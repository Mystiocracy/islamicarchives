1. What the article is about

Only one Qur'anic manuscript carries a date from the first century of the hijra and two from the second, so specialists must date the rest by comparison. The article's authors gather the manuscripts that can be assigned to the first century (roughly the seventh century CE), written in the early ḥijāzī and kufic styles, and give bibliographic references for each so that readers can follow up. It covers only manuscripts of the Qur'an, not Qur'anic inscriptions on rocks or coins, which the site treats on a separate page. The authors call it a working document that will be revised as more manuscripts are published.

The first century is chosen because it is the earliest and most informative period of Qur'an production, even though any end date involves some arbitrariness. The authors point to the end of the century as a time of major developments in Muslim visual culture: Arabic became the official script and language of administration, large building projects were undertaken, and Qur'ans were produced under official patronage.

2. Earlier lists of early manuscripts

  • Adolf Grohmann (about fifty years before the article) compiled one of the first lists of Qur'anic manuscripts attributable to the first century, dating them mainly by comparing the script with dated Arabic papyri. His list had eight entries, though only seven distinct manuscripts: British Museum Or. 2165, Arabe 328a, Topkapı Medina 1a, A. Perg. 2, P. Cair. B.E. 1700, Vat. Ar. 1605, Arabic Palaeography Plate 44 and P. Michaélidès no. 32.
  • Beatrice Gruendler, roughly thirty-five years after Grohmann, gathered published dated Arabic texts of the first century and added three manuscripts: the Mingana palimpsest (Ms. Or. 1287), DAM 01-27.1 (part of Codex Ṣanʿāʾ I) and LNS 19 CAab (part of Or. 2165).
  • Marc-Georges Noseda and François Déroche produced a table of ḥijāzī manuscripts. By Noseda's count they represent about 83% of the Qur'anic text when compared with the King Fuʾād edition. The article's authors note that this table lists only holding institutions, gives only rough indications of content and has no bibliography, and they set out to supply fuller details.

3. How early manuscripts are dated

The article argues that no single method suffices and that dating requires several lines of evidence together. It also rejects the idea that a manuscript can only be dated safely if it has an endowment notice, calling this an unreasonable standard for fragmentary material.

3.1 Literary evidence

  • Ibn al-Nadīm (d. 380/990) says the earliest scripts were those of Makkah, Madīnah, Baṣra and Kūfa, and describes the Makkan and Madinan alifs in terms of a rightward turn, tall strokes and a slight slant. The authors follow an alternative reading, associated with Whelan, in which these three features describe the script as a whole: rightward-leaning alifs, raised ascenders and a slightly inclined form. Because the two cannot be told apart, the label “ḥijāzī” serves as a general name for the earliest script.
  • Yaḥyā ibn Abī Kathīr al-Yamāmī (d. 132/749) reports the order in which aids were added to the muṣḥaf: first dots to distinguish letters such as bāʾ and tāʾ, then larger dots marking verse ends, then markers for the beginnings and ends of sūrahs. Because he states this as a plain observation without taking sides in the debate over dotting, the authors treat it as a credible report from the late first or early second century.
  • From this the authors derive a four-stage relative sequence: (1) no marks at all; (2) letter-distinguishing marks only; (3) marks plus verse-end dots; (4) all of these plus sūrah markers. They note that the stages overlapped and that conservative scribal habits persisted, so manuscripts cannot simply be sorted into them. All the manuscripts in their list have sparse diacritics and some verse separators, and they suggest the very earliest stages (1 and 2) have not survived or have not been found.

3.2 Palaeographic and codicological evidence

  • Déroche's typology divides the ḥijāzī script into four types (Ḥijāzī I–IV), and in later work he dated ḥijāzī Qur'ans to the first century, continuing into the early second, with the kufic script gradually replacing it under the reforms of ʿAbd al-Malik. The article's criticisms: it is unclear whether the small differences between types reflect different scripts, regions or just individual scribes, and the lettering system is a taxonomy detached from the manuscript tradition itself.
  • Noseda's diagram of the development of Arabic script treats kufic as the successor that gradually displaced ḥijāzī from the time of ʿAbd al-Malik. Straightening in inscriptions begins around the 50s of the hijra, and most scholars now place the main ḥijāzī body in the seventh century CE.
  • Codicology. Nearly all early Qur'ans are on parchment (with a handful of exceptions) in codex form; a few use a roll (rotulus) format. Most manuscripts studied by Déroche from the late first to fourth centuries are made of quinions (quires of ten leaves), but this is not universal: Arabe 328c is of quinions while Arabe 328a, of the same period, is of quaternions (eight leaves).
  • Colour. Red ink and coloured decoration appear in the first century: Is. 1615 I and Arabe 328c / Mingana 1572a have red sūrah separators, Or. 2165 has red headings (probably later additions), and TIEM ŞE 362 uses three colours to make a geometric pattern. The authors use these examples against the idea that any decorated Qur'an must be late.

3.3 Art-historical evidence

Ornament is compared with dated buildings. DAM 20-33.1 has been linked to decoration in the Dome of the Rock, the Great Mosques of Ṣanʿāʾ and Damascus and related mosaics; Hans-Caspar Graf von Bothmer dated it to about 710–715 CE under al-Walīd. TIEM ŞE 321 was dated by Déroche to after 72/691–692 and probably the last quarter of the first century, on the basis of its illumination, script, ten-leaf quires and resemblance to the mosaics of the Dome of the Rock and the Umayyad Mosque. Critics have charged the method with circularity, since the comparison material is mostly Umayyad.

3.4 Radiocarbon dating

The authors count nineteen Qur'anic manuscripts successfully dated by radiocarbon (see their radiocarbon page), eight of which appear in their table. The advantage is that it is independent of assumptions about script history; the drawback is wide date ranges for some manuscripts. The St Petersburg E20 and the Samarqand codices, both wrongly attributed to ʿUthmān, have ranges of 225 and 260 years. Others are tighter. The authors quote a view that, thanks to newly found documents and a better understanding of Umayyad-era changes in the script, most ḥijāzī Qur'ans are now seen as first-century.

4. Certainty and the burden of proof

The article takes issue with extreme caution in dating. It cites Arberry's assignment of Is. 1615 I to the fourth century as an example: a date called “conservative” is still wrong if the manuscript is much earlier, and errors in either direction distort the chronology of the script and thus the dating of other manuscripts. It agrees with Blair that no absolute method exists for dating Qur'ans before the ninth century CE, but argues that historical certainty is not the same as mathematical proof, and that when palaeography and radiocarbon agree (as for Mingana 1572a) a first-century attribution is well supported. As an analogy it points to early Arab-Byzantine coinage, where a methodological advance recently allowed secure dates for series that had long been undated.

5. Provenance and major discoveries

The authors observe that many manuscripts on their list are in Western collections and none originated there, and that the lack of any from present-day Saudi Arabia probably reflects a lack of searching and cataloguing rather than a true absence. The article discusses the history of collecting in a pointed way. Its account, which readers should weigh against other scholarship, claims the following:

  • Gotthelf Bergsträsser and Otto Pretzl photographed about 15,000 images of early Qur'an manuscripts on their travels in Muslim lands, and these photographs were not shared with their hosts. Anton Spitaler later inherited them and, the article says, concealed them for over fifty years before passing them to Angelika Neuwirth, who leads the Corpus Coranicum project.
  • A German team conserving the Ṣanʿāʾ manuscripts photographed them, and the article reports an attempt to take about 35,000 images out of Yemen that was blocked until German diplomats intervened. Yemen holds the master microfilms and the copyright.
  • Damascus. After a fire at the Great Mosque in 1893, a forgotten store of used Qur'an manuscripts was found; the surviving material was sent to Istanbul and rediscovered by two French scholars in 1963 as “Papers from Damascus”. Déroche estimated about 210,000 folios, mostly Qur'ans.
  • Ṣanʿāʾ. Rain damage to the Western Library of the Great Mosque in 1965 led to the discovery of a sealed storeroom full of used manuscripts, almost all Qur'anic. Some were removed and sold improperly, and the rest were moved to the National Museum in 1972. The cache comprises nearly 1,000 distinct Qur'ans in about 15,000 parchment fragments, of which only a small part has been published.

6. How much of the Qur'an survives in these manuscripts

To measure the value of the manuscripts, the authors use the printed Madinah muṣḥaf (Ḥafṣ reading) as the yardstick, as Noseda used the King Fuʾād edition. Points they make:

  • The Madinah muṣḥaf runs to 604 pages of 15 lines, with 6,236 verses; the first juzʾ is 21 pages and the last 23.
  • The length of sūrahs falls off roughly exponentially: half the text is reached at al-Kahf (18) and about 90% by al-Mujādila (58), so the last 56 sūrahs make up only about 10% of the text. This means that finding the long early sūrahs has an outsized effect on total coverage.
  • Their Table II lists, sūrah by sūrah, which manuscripts contain it, which verses, the number of unique verses attested and the percentage of the sūrah. In the part of the table I could read, most of sūrahs 3 to 41 are shown as 100% attested, with al-Baqara at about 60.8% (174 of 286 verses) and al-Fātiḥa at 100%. Heavily fragmentary items such as P. Michaélidès 32 are counted only for the sūrahs they contain, not for verse totals.
Limitation. The copy of the article I could retrieve ended partway through Table II (around sūrah 41), so the figures for sūrahs 42–114, the totals and the article's closing conclusions are not summarized here. Please consult the original for those, and verify all figures on this page against it.

8. Further manuscripts and related pages

The article also lists, without full table entries, other early or first/second-century manuscripts, grouped by country:

  • Yemen: DAM 01-28.1 (page), DAM 01-30.1 (page), DAM 01-18.3 (page), DAM 01-32.1 (page), DAM 01-29.2 (page) and DAM 01-32.2.
  • Turkey: Topkapı Medina 1a, several TIEM manuscripts and TIEM ŞE 12995.
  • Austria: A. Perg. 186, A. Perg. 202 and Mixt. 917.
  • Others: Tunisia (Ms. R 38, R 119, P 511), the USA, Egypt, Britain (including many Khalili Collection items), France, Ireland (Is. 1404) and the multi-library Codex Amrensis 1, plus many pieces sold at Sotheby's and Christie's auctions.
  • The Mingana palimpsest (Ms. Or. 1287), whose lower text is first-century, is on its own page; the authors say it will be added to the table later.
  • Qur'ans popularly attributed to ʿUthmān or ʿAlī: Samarqand, St Petersburg, Topkapı, TIEM, al-Ḥusayn Mosque and Dār al-Kutub in Cairo, and the Qur'an attributed to ʿAlī in Ṣanʿāʾ.
  • Kodex Wetzstein II 1913 (Berlin), 210 folios and little studied, is on its own page. The article also mentions a 270-folio ḥijāzī codex on display at the Museum of Islamic Art in Cairo that has received almost no scholarly notice.

  • François Déroche, Qur'ans of the Umayyads: A First Overview (Leiden: Brill, 2014). Closest in subject to the article: first-century and early-eighth-century Qur'ans.
  • François Déroche, Catalogue des manuscrits arabes. Deuxième partie: Manuscrits musulmans. Tome I/1: Les manuscrits du Coran, aux origines de la calligraphie coranique (Paris: Bibliothèque nationale, 1983). The source of the Ḥijāzī I–IV typology.

  • Sheila S. Blair, Islamic Calligraphy (Edinburgh: Edinburgh University Press, 2006).
  • Muhammad Mustafa al-Aʿẓamī, The History of the Qurʾānic Text from Revelation to Compilation: A Comparative Study with the Old and New Testaments (Leicester: UK Islamic Academy, 2003).
  • Bayard Dodge (trans.), The Fihrist of al-Nadīm: A Tenth-Century Survey of Muslim Culture (New York: Columbia University Press, 1970).
  • A. J. Arberry, The Koran Illuminated: A Handlist of the Korans in the Chester Beatty Library (Dublin: Hodges Figgis, 1967).
  • Gerd-R. Puin, “Observations on Early Qurʾān Manuscripts in Ṣanʿāʾ,” in Stefan Wild (ed.), The Qurʾān as Text (Leiden: Brill, 1996).

These works disagree with one another, and sometimes with the article, on dating and interpretation. Reading across them is the best way to evaluate the claims made here.
