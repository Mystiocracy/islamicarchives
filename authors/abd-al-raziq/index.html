ʿAlī ʿAbd al-Rāziq

علي عبد الرازق

1888–1966 · Egypt · Azhari scholar, judge, political theorist, minister

ʿAlī ʿAbd al-Rāziq was born in 1888 in Minya province in Upper Egypt, into a wealthy landowning family with close ties to the liberal politics of the day. His father, Ḥasan ʿAbd al-Rāziq, was among the founders of the Umma Party in 1907, and his elder brother Muṣṭafā, a student of Muḥammad ʿAbduh, later became a philosopher and Shaykh of al-Azhar. ʿAlī received his ʿālim degree from al-Azhar in 1911 and in 1912 went to Oxford to study economics and political science. The First World War cut his stay short.

On his return he became a judge in the sharīʿa court at Manṣūra. In 1925, shortly after the Turkish Republic abolished the caliphate and while several rulers were competing for the title, he published al-Islām wa-uṣūl al-ḥukm. In it he argued that neither the Qurʾān nor the Sunna requires a caliphate, that there was never a real consensus on its necessity, and that the Prophet’s mission was spiritual rather than the founding of a state. Muslims, he concluded, were free to choose whatever form of government served their welfare.

The book caused a storm. A council of senior Azhari scholars condemned it, stripped him of his ʿālim status and dismissed him from his judgeship, and refutations appeared by Muḥammad al-Khiḍr Ḥusayn, Muḥammad Bakhīt al-Muṭīʿī and Rashīd Riḍā. Later he was rehabilitated, published al-Ijmāʿ fī al-sharīʿa al-islāmiyya (1947), and twice served as Minister of Endowments.

ʿAbd al-Rāziq died in 1966. He wrote little else, but his book is still treated as the classic statement of the case that Islam prescribes no particular political system, and it remains central to Muslim debates about religion and the state.
