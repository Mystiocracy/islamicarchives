Rifāʿa Rāfiʿ al-Ṭahṭāwī was born in 1801 at Ṭahṭā in Upper Egypt, into a family of religious scholars whose fortunes had declined. He studied at al-Azhar in Cairo, where his teacher Ḥasan al-ʿAṭṭār, himself curious about European learning, encouraged his interest in subjects beyond the standard curriculum.

In 1826 Muḥammad ʿAlī Pasha sent a large educational mission to Paris, and al-Ṭahṭāwī went with it as its imam. He stayed five years, learned French, and read widely in history, geography, law and the political writers of the Enlightenment. His account of the journey, *Takhlīṣ al-ibrīz fī talkhīṣ Bārīz* (1834), described French institutions, the constitutional charter and Parisian customs for Egyptian readers, and became one of the founding texts of the Arabic encounter with modern Europe.

Back in Egypt he directed the School of Languages (founded 1835), supervised the translation of some two thousand European works, and edited the official gazette. Out of favour under ʿAbbās I, he spent several years in Khartoum before returning to educational posts under Saʿīd and Ismāʿīl. In his later books, notably *Manāhij al-albāb al-Miṣriyya* (1869) and *al-Murshid al-amīn li-l-banāt wa-l-banīn* (1872), he argued that the sharīʿa was compatible with the natural law underlying European codes, spoke of love of the homeland (*waṭan*) as a civic virtue, and called for the education of girls as well as boys.

Al-Ṭahṭāwī died in Cairo in 1873. He is usually regarded as the first major figure of the Arab *nahḍa*. His attempt to reconcile Azhari learning with European political and scientific thought anticipated the questions taken up by later reformers such as Muḥammad ʿAbduh.
