Ḥasan al-Bannā was born in 1906 in al-Maḥmūdiyya in the Nile Delta. His father, Aḥmad ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Bannā, was a watch-repairer and religious scholar who arranged the ḥadīth collection of Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal. As a boy Ḥasan joined religious societies and the Ḥaṣāfiyya Sufi order, then trained as a teacher in Damanhūr and at Dār al-ʿUlūm in Cairo, graduating in 1927. In Cairo he read *al-Manār* and attended gatherings around Rashīd Riḍā.

Posted as a primary-school teacher to Ismāʿīliyya, the headquarters of the British-run Suez Canal Company, he founded the Society of the Muslim Brothers (*Jamʿiyyat al-Ikhwān al-Muslimīn*) in 1928. He moved its centre to Cairo in 1932, and by the late 1940s it had become a mass movement with hundreds of thousands of members, a network of branches, schools, clinics and businesses, and a secret paramilitary wing.

Al-Bannā set out his ideas mainly in short epistles (*rasāʾil*) and speeches, later gathered in *Majmūʿat rasāʾil al-Imām al-Shahīd*. He presented Islam as a complete system covering worship, morality, law, economy and government, and argued that Egypt's problems came from imitating the West and from colonial rule. His remedy was gradual: reform of the individual, then the family and society, and finally a government run on Islamic principles. His letter *Naḥwa al-nūr* urged the king and ruling elite to adopt such a programme, and his *Risālat al-taʿālīm* laid down the principles and duties of members.

After members of the Brotherhood were linked to political violence, the government dissolved the society in December 1948. Weeks later Prime Minister al-Nuqrāshī was assassinated, and al-Bannā himself was shot dead in Cairo in February 1949. The movement he founded spread across the Muslim world and is the model for much of modern Islamist activism.
